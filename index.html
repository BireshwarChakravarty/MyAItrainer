    This module explains what is going on inside tools like ChatGPT and Copilot. Once you understand that, it's much easier to judge what to trust them with.

    Start with a working definition

    Artificial intelligence (AI) is software that does tasks we normally think need human judgement: recognising patterns, understanding language, making predictions, or producing writing.

    The important word is judgement. A spreadsheet formula that adds a column is not AI. It follows an exact instruction someone wrote. AI is different because it has learned how to do something from examples, rather than being told every step.

    The family tree

    You will hear several terms used as if they mean the same thing. They are nested, like boxes inside boxes:

    Artificial IntelligenceThe whole field: any software that mimics human judgement.
    Machine Learning (ML)AI that learns patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. Example: a demand forecast that learns seasonality from three years of sales.
    Deep LearningML using large "neural networks". Powers image recognition, voice and reading scanned invoices.
    Generative AI & Large Language Models (LLMs)Deep learning that creates new text, tables, code or images. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot are all built on LLMs.

    Two kinds of AI you will meet at work

    For a procurement and audit role, almost everything falls into one of two groups. Knowing which one you are dealing with tells you what to expect from it.

    Typical outputA number, a score, a flagA draft, a summary, an explanation, a formula
    Examples in your worldDemand forecasting · flagging unusual invoices · predicting supplier late delivery · scoring which stock locations to count nextWriting a variance summary · drafting an email to a warehouse manager · writing an Excel formula · summarising a contract clause

    How a chatbot actually produces an answer

    If you only remember one thing from this module, make it this. A large language model works in four steps:

    1. It read an enormous amount of text. During training, the model processed a large share of the public internet, books and other documents. It did not memorise them like a database. It learned the patterns of how words, ideas and facts tend to go together.
    2. It breaks your message into "tokens". A token is a chunk of text, roughly three-quarters of a word. "Reconciliation" might be two or three tokens. This is also how usage and limits are measured.
    3. It predicts the next token, over and over. Given everything so far, it calculates which token is most likely to come next, picks one, adds it, and repeats. A whole answer is built one small piece at a time.
    4. It was coached to be helpful. After the initial training, people rated its answers so it learned to follow instructions, be polite and refuse harmful requests. That is why it behaves like an assistant rather than an autocomplete.

    Why this matters for your work

    Once you see that the model is predicting likely words, three practical consequences follow. Keep these in mind every time you use AI.

    1. Fluent is not the same as correct

    The model is optimised to produce text that sounds right. If it doesn't know, it often still produces a confident, plausible answer. This is called a hallucination. Module 3 shows you how to catch them.

    2. It only knows what you give it

    It has never seen your ERP, your stock ledger or your supplier contracts. Unless you paste or upload data, or your company connects it to internal systems, it is guessing about your business.

    3. Words are its strength, sums are not

    It's good with language, structure and explanations. Exact arithmetic across many rows is weaker, unless the tool writes and runs code to do the maths (Module 5 explains how to make it do that).

    The best mental model: a brilliant new hire on their first day.

    They have read every textbook and work very fast. But they know nothing about your company, your seven warehouses or your suppliers. They rarely say "I don't know" unless you tell them it is allowed. They need clear instructions, and you check their work before it goes to your manager. Treat AI exactly like that and you will rarely be caught out.

    What AI is good and bad at, for an auditor

    Hand these to AI

    • Writing and explaining Excel formulas
    • Turning a messy list of findings into a clear report
    • Summarising long documents (SOPs, contracts, policies)
    • Suggesting what to check and where errors usually hide
    • Classifying free-text fields, such as adjustment reasons
    • Drafting emails, agendas and meeting notes
    • Explaining a concept you don't understand yet

    Keep these with you

    • Final numbers that go in a report (verify every one)
    • Deciding whether something is fraud or an error
    • Approving stock adjustments or payments
    • Anything involving confidential data in an unapproved tool
    • Judgement calls about people
    • Knowing your company's actual policies (unless you give them)

    The four layers

    Think of them as moving from "general and flexible" to "specialised and automatic".

    1. Layer 1Chat assistantsA general assistant you talk to in a browser or app. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot Chat.
    2. Layer 2AI inside your appsThe Copilot or Gemini button inside Excel, Outlook, Teams, Word, Google Sheets.
    3. Layer 3AI in business systemsFeatures built into your ERP, WMS or procurement suite: invoice matching, spend classification, demand forecasts.
    4. Layer 4Automation & agentsFlows and agents that run multi-step tasks on a schedule or trigger, such as Power Automate.

    Layer 1 · Chat assistants

    The main general-purpose assistants are ChatGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google) and Microsoft Copilot. For your purposes they are more alike than different. All of them can explain concepts, write and fix formulas, draft documents, and analyse uploaded files. Recent reviews generally describe ChatGPT as the broadest all-rounder, Claude as strong for long documents and careful writing, Copilot as the natural fit if your company runs on Microsoft 365, and Gemini as the fit for Google Workspace.

    The best one for you is usually the one your company has approved, because that version comes with business data protections. More on that in Module 3.

    Layer 2 · AI inside the apps you already use

    This is where an auditor gets the most value, because your data already lives there.

    • Copilot in Excel can create formula columns, highlight, sort and filter, build PivotTables and charts, and explain what is in a sheet. In early 2026 Microsoft made Agent Mode generally available in Excel for Windows, Mac and the web. You describe a multi-step job in plain English ("build a summary sheet comparing variance by warehouse with a chart") and it builds and edits the workbook step by step.
    • The =COPILOT() function lets you use a plain-English prompt inside a cell. It is useful for classifying text, but Microsoft itself warns not to use it for tasks needing accuracy or reproducibility, such as calculations. Module 7 covers exactly when to use it.
    • Copilot in Outlook and Teams summarises long email threads and meetings, and drafts replies.
    • Gemini in Google Sheets and Gmail offers similar features for Google Workspace companies.

    Layer 3 · AI in your business systems

    ERP, WMS and procurement platforms (SAP, Oracle, Microsoft Dynamics, Coupa and others) increasingly include AI features: automatic three-way matching, duplicate invoice detection, spend classification, risk-based cycle count suggestions. You typically don't configure these yourself, but you should know whether your company has them, because they change what you need to check manually.

    Layer 4 · Automation and agents

    Tools like Microsoft Power Automate run a sequence of steps on a schedule ("every Monday at 7am, collect the seven warehouse files, refresh the report, email me the exceptions"). AI agents go a step further and can plan and carry out multi-step tasks with some independence. You'll build up to this in Module 10.

    Using AI safely

    Two things an auditor can't get wrong are keeping sensitive data safe and making sure the numbers are correct. This module covers habits for both.

    About 25 minutes. Level: Beginner.

    After this module you should be able to:

    • Decide in seconds whether data is safe to share with an AI tool
    • Anonymise a dataset so you can still get help without exposing anything sensitive
    • Use a five-step check to catch AI mistakes before they reach a report

    Why this comes before everything else

    Two examples:

    • In a January 2025 survey of employees at large US companies (TELUS Digital), 57% admitted entering confidential company data into public AI tools. Many used personal accounts their employers couldn't see or control.
    • In February 2024, a Canadian tribunal ruled that Air Canada was responsible for incorrect refund information its website chatbot invented. "The AI said it" was not accepted as a defence.

    The lesson for you: you are responsible for what you put into AI, and for what you take out of it. Neither is hard to manage once you have the habits.

    Part 1 · What data is safe to share?

    Use a simple traffic-light system. Always check your company's own AI policy first; it overrides anything here.

    Green
    Fine for any approved tool

    • General questions ("what is a GRN?")
    • Formula help using made-up column names
    • Fake or sample data you invented
    • Public information (published prices, news)
    • Your own writing to polish, with no sensitive details

    Amber
    Only in your company-approved tool

    • Internal stock or variance data
    • Warehouse codes, SKU lists, internal reports
    • Aggregated spend by category
    • Anonymised supplier data
    • Internal SOPs and process documents

    Red
    Never, unless policy explicitly allows

    • Supplier contract prices and terms
    • Bank details, tax IDs, payment info
    • Employee names linked to performance or errors
    • Personal data of any person
    • Passwords, system logins
    • Anything under NDA, unreleased financials, suspected-fraud details
    A simple test

    If you would not email it to someone outside the company, do not paste it into a tool your company has not approved.

    Anonymise: get the help without the risk

    Most of the time AI only needs the structure of your data (the columns and the kind of values) to help you. Replace the sensitive parts before you share.

    Before · real data
    SupplierUnit priceApprover
    Apex Packaging Ltd4.37R. Mehta
    Northline Pallets11.90S. Iyer
    After · safe to share
    SupplierUnit priceApprover
    Supplier A10.00Approver 1
    Supplier B27.23Approver 2

    Names replaced with codes; prices scaled by the same factor so patterns still show.

    Even better: ask AI for the method (a formula, a set of steps) using fake data, then apply it yourself to the real data in Excel. The sensitive data never leaves your machine.

    Part 2 · Catching AI mistakes

    AI errors in data work usually look like one of these:

    • Invented numbers: a total or percentage that doesn't match your data
    • Dropped rows: it quietly analysed only part of a large table
    • Invented functions: an Excel function that doesn't exist, or exists only in newer versions
    • Confident policy claims: "standard practice is…" with nothing to back it
    • Made-up sources: references or links that don't exist

    Use this five-step check every time AI output will feed into something other people see. We call it "Tie it out", the same way an auditor ties a figure back to source.

    1. Tie the totals. Does the row count match? Does the grand total match what Excel's SUM gives you? This alone catches most errors.
    2. Spot-check 5–10 rows by hand. Pick a few at random, plus the biggest and smallest. Are they right?
    3. Ask "how did you get this?" Make the AI show its method or formula. Vague answers are a warning sign.
    4. Ask it to check itself. "Review your answer for errors and list anything you are unsure about." It often catches some of its own mistakes.
    5. Trace to the system of record. Audit evidence must come from the ERP, WMS or source documents, never from the AI's output alone.
    Remember

    AI drafts, you decide. AI can find, sort, suggest and write. A person confirms the facts and owns the conclusion.

    Writing good prompts

    How useful an AI answer is depends mostly on how clearly you ask. This module gives you a five-part checklist for writing a request.

    About 30 minutes. Level: Beginner to intermediate.

    After this module you should be able to:

    • Write a complete prompt using the five-part Pick Ticket method
    • Turn a weak, one-line request into one that gets a usable first answer
    • Use follow-up questions to improve an answer rather than starting over

    Why most prompts disappoint

    People type a short request like "analyse this data" and get a generic, vague answer. That isn't the AI being weak. A new hire given an instruction that short would have to guess too.

    A prompt is simply the instruction you give the AI. Good prompts don't need special tricks or magic words. They need the same things any clear work instruction needs.

    The Pick Ticket method

    A pick ticket tells a picker exactly what to collect, from where, how many, and how to pack it. Nobody has to guess. A good prompt does the same. It has five fields:

    FieldQuestion it answersExample
    RoleWhoWho should the AI act as?"You are an experienced inventory auditor for a multi-warehouse distributor."
    TaskWhatWhat exactly do you want done?"Identify the five SKUs with the largest stock variance and suggest likely causes."
    ContextBackgroundWhat does it need to know to do this well?"We run 7 warehouses. Variance = system qty minus counted qty. Tolerance is ±2%. This is October's cycle count data."
    FormatPack asWhat should the answer look like?"A table with columns: SKU, Warehouse, Variance %, Likely cause, Suggested check. Then 3 bullet points of summary."
    ChecksQuality controlWhat rules and checks must it follow?"Only use the data provided. If something is unclear, ask me. State the row count you analysed."

    You don't need all five every time. A quick question might need just TASK. But when an answer disappoints, the missing field is almost always CONTEXT or FORMAT.

    Same request, two ways

    Weak prompt

    Look at this stock data and tell me what's wrong.

    Likely result: a generic list of "possible data quality issues", with no priorities and no link to your tolerances or warehouses.

    Pick Ticket prompt

    You are an experienced inventory auditor. Below is October cycle count data from our 7 warehouses (columns: Warehouse, SKU, System_Qty, Counted_Qty, Unit_Cost). Our tolerance is ±2% per line. Find every line outside tolerance, rank them by value of variance, and for the top 10 suggest the most likely cause and what I should check first. Return a table, then a 3-line summary. Use only this data and tell me how many rows you analysed.

    Your prompt

    Six habits that improve results

    1. Be specific with numbers and names. "Variance above 2%" beats "big variances". "Warehouses 1–7" beats "our sites".
    2. Show an example of what you want. Paste one row of the output you expect, or a past report you liked. AI copies examples very well.
    3. Let it ask you questions first. End with: "Before you start, ask me up to 3 questions about anything unclear." This usually makes the answer noticeably better.
    4. Break big jobs into steps. First "summarise the data", then "find exceptions", then "draft the findings". Check each step before the next.
    5. Give it permission to say "I don't know". Add: "If the data doesn't support an answer, say so rather than guessing." This reduces hallucinations.
    6. Treat it as a conversation. The first answer is a draft. Improve it with follow-ups instead of starting a new chat.

    Follow-up phrases worth memorising

    When the answer is…Say this
    Too longCut this to 5 bullet points a busy manager can read in 30 seconds.
    Too technicalExplain this as if I'm new to the job. Use a warehouse example.
    Possibly wrongWhat assumptions did you make? What could be wrong with this answer?
    Too genericMake this specific to a company with 7 warehouses and a ±2% tolerance.
    Only one ideaGive me 3 different options with pros and cons for each.
    Good, want to reuseTurn what we just did into a reusable prompt template with [brackets] for the parts that change.
    Tip

    Save your standing context once. Most assistants let you set custom instructions or create a Project. Put in: your role, your seven warehouses and their codes, your tolerance rules, your preferred report format. Every chat then starts already knowing your job.

    I'm an assistant purchase manager at a supply chain company. I audit inventory, purchasing and invoice data across 7 warehouses (codes WH01–WH07).
- Stock variance = system quantity minus physical count. Our line tolerance is ±[2]% or ±[value].
- Key systems: [ERP name], [WMS name]. I mainly work in Excel.
- When I share data: confirm the row count, use only the data given, and say clearly if something can't be determined.
- Prefer tables for results and short bullet summaries. Use plain business English.
- When suggesting Excel formulas, assume Microsoft 365 and explain each part briefly.

    Summary

    • A good prompt is a clear work instruction: Role, Task, Context, Format, Checks.
    • When results disappoint, add context or specify the format.
    • Let AI ask you questions, and give it permission to say "I don't know".
    • Improve answers with follow-ups. Save your standing context in custom instructions.

    Working with data

    Asking AI about data needs more care than asking it to write an email. This module covers how to give it your data and how to check the numbers that come back.

    Three ways to give AI your data

    MethodBest forWatch out for
    Paste a small table into the chatUp to about 50 rows. Quick questions, formula help, examplesLarger pastes can get truncated or misread. Arithmetic by word prediction is unreliable.
    Upload a file to an assistant with data analysisHundreds to tens of thousands of rows. One-off analysis, finding patterns, chartsCheck it read every sheet and every row. Ask it to show the code or steps it ran.

    Prepare your data: the clean-table checklist

    AI, Excel formulas, PivotTables and Power Query all struggle with the same things. Ten minutes of tidying saves an hour of confusion. The aim is a clean table:

    Do

    • One header row, with clear names: System_Qty, not Qty
    • One row per record (one line per SKU per location)
    • One type of data per column: numbers as numbers, dates as dates
    • Same units throughout. Note them in the header: Qty_EA vs Qty_CASE
    • A column saying which warehouse each row belongs to
    • Format it as an Excel Table (Ctrl+T)

    Avoid

    • Merged cells and multi-row headers
    • Subtotal or total rows mixed in with data
    • Blank rows or columns used as spacing
    • Colour as the only way of marking something
    • Notes typed into number columns ("approx 40")
    • Seven warehouses on seven differently-laid-out tabs

    Give it a data dictionary

    A data dictionary is a short description of each column. It's the most effective way to stop AI misreading your data. Put it at the top of your prompt.

    Columns in the attached file:
- WH: warehouse code, WH01 to WH07
- SKU: item code
- UOM: unit of measure (EA = each, CS = case of 12)
- Sys_Qty: quantity in the WMS at count time, in UOM
- Cnt_Qty: physically counted quantity, in UOM
- Unit_Cost: standard cost per EA in USD
- Count_Date: date of the cycle count (DD/MM/YYYY)
- Counter_ID: anonymised counter code
Notes: a negative variance means stock is missing. Rows where UOM = CS must be multiplied by 12 before comparing to EA.

    Ask for the method, not only the answer

    This is the most important habit in this module. Instead of "what's the total variance?", ask the AI to give you the formula, the steps or the code. Three benefits:

    • You can check it. A formula is either right or wrong. A number on its own is a black box.
    • You can reuse it. Next month, apply the same formula to new data without AI at all.
    • Your data stays put. You can get the formula using fake data, then apply it to the real thing.
    I have an Excel Table called Counts with columns WH, SKU, Sys_Qty, Cnt_Qty, Unit_Cost.
Write me formulas (Microsoft 365) for new columns:
1. Var_Qty (counted minus system)
2. Var_Pct (variance as % of system qty, handling system qty = 0 safely)
3. Var_Value (variance quantity × unit cost)
4. Out_Of_Tolerance ("YES" if absolute Var_Pct is over 2%, else "NO")

    Follow the flow: where errors creep in

    Why stock records and counts disagree

    Before calling something a loss, rule out the common, innocent causes. These are the usual suspects in multi-site networks:

    CauseWhat it looks like in data
    Timing gapsCount taken before a receipt or pick was posted. Variance reverses the next day.
    System sync delaysWMS and ERP disagree for the same SKU and date.
    Unit-of-measure mismatchVariance is an exact multiple (×6, ×12, ×24): cases counted as eaches or vice versa.
    Transfers in transitStock leaves WH02 but hasn't been received at WH05. One shows a loss, the other a gain.
    Inconsistent receivingOver- or under-receipts at one dock, often the same supplier or shift.

    Count smarter with ABC classification

    Not all items deserve equal attention. ABC classification ranks SKUs by annual value (cost × usage). Typically a small share of SKUs (the A items, often around 20%) make up most of the value (often around 80%). A common pattern is to count A items most often (for example monthly), B items less often, and C items least. Some teams also rank by velocity or risk, not just value.     The red flag checklist

    These patterns are widely cited by audit and fraud specialists. A red flag is a reason to look closer, not proof of wrongdoing. Most turn out to be process errors.

    Invoices & payments

    • Same supplier, amount and date on two invoices
    • Near-duplicates: invoice number with a suffix ("4471-A"), or digits transposed
    • Invoices without a PO
    • Invoice quantity higher than goods received
    • Round-number invoices from service suppliers
    • PO created after the invoice date

    Purchasing

    • Split POs: several orders just under an approval limit, same supplier, close dates
    • Spend with non-approved suppliers ("maverick spend")
    • Prices above contract or above the last PO
    • Repeated awards to one supplier without justification
    • Frequent change orders on the same supplier

    Vendor master

    • Duplicate suppliers (same name, address, tax ID or bank account)
    • Missing tax ID or contact details
    • Supplier address or phone matching an employee's
    • PO box-only addresses
    • Recently created supplier with large, fast payments

    Stock & adjustments

    • Repeated adjustments on the same SKU or by the same user
    • Adjustments just before or after a count
    • Adjustments outside working hours
    • Negative stock balances
    • Write-offs concentrated on high-value, portable items

    Summary

    • Trace every odd number back to the step that created it: PO, GRN, putaway, invoice, payment, count.
    • Track IRA, value-weighted and gross variance per warehouse, against each warehouse's own trend.
    • Rule out timing, UOM, transfers and mis-slotting before calling anything a loss.
    • Red flags are leads, not conclusions.

    Automation

    Once a check works and you trust the results, you can stop doing it by hand. This module covers what's worth automating, how to go about it, and where AI agents fit in.

    About 35 minutes. Level: Advanced.

    After this module you should be able to:

    • Tell the difference between automation and AI, and when you need each
    • Score a task to decide whether it's worth automating

    Automation and AI are different things

    Automation follows rules you define: "every Monday, copy these files, refresh this report, email it". It is exact and predictable. AI handles judgement and language: "classify these comments", "summarise these exceptions". The most useful workflows combine both: automation moves and calculates the data, AI handles the parts that need reading or writing, and you review the result.

    The automation ladder

    Climb one rung at a time. Each rung is valuable on its own, and each one makes the next easier.

    1. Saved prompts and templates. Your best prompts saved and reused (the prompt library is a start). Zero setup.
    2. Refreshable workbooks. Power Query combine plus formulas and PivotTables. Monthly work drops from hours to a refresh and a review.
    3. Scheduled flows. Power Automate (or similar) moves files, triggers refreshes and sends emails on a schedule or when a file arrives.
    4. AI steps inside flows. A flow that also asks AI to classify, summarise or draft, such as writing the first draft of the weekly exception email.
    5. Agents with approvals. AI that plans and carries out several steps toward a goal, pausing for your approval before anything important happens.

    AI agents: what they are and what's coming

    An AI agent is AI that doesn't just answer a question but works toward a goal: it plans steps, uses tools (files, systems, email), checks results and continues. Excel's Agent Mode, which builds a workbook in several steps, is a simple example you can use today.

    Analysts expect agents to spread quickly in procurement. Gartner's 2026 outlook describes procurement moving toward an "AI-first" model in which agents carry out tasks such as compliance checks and payments within strict governance guardrails. It also forecasts that by 2030, half of cross-functional supply chain management solutions will use intelligent agents to execute decisions autonomously. Treat such forecasts as direction, not certainty, but the direction is clear: knowing how to supervise AI will become part of your job.

    Guardrails for any automation or agent

    Human approval for anything that changes records

    Stock adjustments, payments, vendor changes and emails to suppliers always need a person to approve. Inventory specialists specifically caution against autonomous adjustments without policy controls.

    Thresholds

    Let automation handle small, clear-cut items; route anything above a value or below a confidence level to a person.

    Audit trail

    Every automated action should be logged: what it did, when, on what data, and who approved. As an auditor, you will appreciate this more than most.

    Failure alerts

    A flow that silently stops is worse than no flow. Make sure failures email you.

    Working with IT

    For anything beyond your own workbooks, involve IT early. They will want to know: what data, which systems, who has access, and what happens if it fails. Have AI help you write a clear one-page request.

    Help me write a one-page automation request for our IT team.

Include sections: Business problem, Proposed solution, Data and access needed, Risks and controls (approvals, audit log, failure alerts), Expected time saved, Success measure. Keep it under 400 words.

    Summary

    • Automation follows rules; AI handles judgement and language. Combine them.
    • Climb the ladder: saved prompts → refreshable workbooks → scheduled flows → AI steps → agents.
    • Automate frequent, rule-based tasks with standard inputs first.
    • Approvals, thresholds, audit trails and failure alerts are non-negotiable.

    Six technologies worth understanding

    Predictive

    Anomaly detection

    The system learns what "normal" looks like (typical receipts per day, typical adjustments per shift, a supplier's usual invoice pattern) and flags what deviates. No one writes a rule for each case. Good tools present flags as leads for review, not accusations.

    Predictive

    Demand forecasting

    Learns patterns such as seasonality, trends and promotions from sales history to predict future demand per SKU and warehouse. Directly affects purchasing quantities and safety stock.

    Vision & documents

    Document AI (OCR+)

    Reads scanned invoices, delivery notes and GRNs, and extracts fields like invoice number, date, lines and totals. This powers automatic three-way matching.

    Vision

    Computer vision counting

    Cameras, drones or robots scan racks and count stock continuously rather than in periodic cycle counts. Gives a near-live view of inventory, mostly in large or high-value sites.

    Generative

    RAG: AI that reads your documents

    "Retrieval-augmented generation": before answering, the AI searches your company documents (SOPs, contracts, policies) and answers from them, citing sources. This is how "ask our procurement policy" assistants work, and it greatly reduces made-up answers.

    Generative

    Custom assistants and agents

    An assistant preconfigured with your instructions and files (a Custom GPT, Claude Project, Copilot agent or Gemini Gem). For example, an "Audit helper" loaded with your SOPs, data dictionary and report template.

    How to judge whether an AI tool is accurate

    Vendors often quote one accuracy figure. For audit tools that flag problems, you need two:

    Precision

    Of everything it flagged, how much was really a problem? Low precision means your team wastes time on false alarms and starts ignoring the tool.

    Recall

    Of all the real problems, how many did it catch? Low recall means real issues slip through while everyone assumes they're covered.

    Questions to ask any AI vendor

    Whether it's a new procurement platform, a WMS add-on or an AP automation tool, these questions separate real capability from marketing:

    Accuracy

    • What are the precision and recall on data like ours? How was it measured?
    • How much historical data does it need? (Some invoice-matching tools cite 6–12 months.)
    • Can we run a pilot on our own data before committing?

    Explainability & control

    • When it flags something, does it show why?
    • Can we set thresholds and approval rules?
    • Is every automated action logged in an audit trail?

    Data & security

    • Where is our data stored? Is it used to train models for other customers?
    • What security certifications do you hold?
    • Who can access our data on your side?

    Fit

    • Does it integrate with our ERP and WMS? How?
    • What does our team have to do differently day to day?
    • What does it cost in year two and three, not just year one?
    Be careful with vendor figures

    Much of what's written about AI in procurement comes from vendors. Figures like "99% detection" or "60% less counting labour" are often unverified or measured in ideal conditions. Ask for the source, and test on your own data.

    Build your own audit helper

    A practical advanced step: create a custom assistant (if your tool supports Projects, GPTs, Gems or Copilot agents) that already knows your audit work.

    1. Gather files (anonymised where needed): your data dictionary, audit SOPs, tolerance rules, finding template, a good past report.

    Where things are heading

    • Adoption is broad but shallow. EFESO's 2026 research found only about 5% of procurement organisations have truly scaled generative AI. People who can turn experiments into reliable routines are in demand.
    • Agents are arriving in procurement. Gartner forecasts agentic AI spending in supply chain software rising from under $2 billion in 2025 to about $53 billion by 2030 (as reported in trade press).
    • Roles are shifting. In a Gartner survey of supply chain leaders, 55% expect agentic AI to reduce hiring for entry-level positions. Routine checking will be automated; supervising, verifying and interpreting will be valued more.

    Summary

    • Know the main technologies: anomaly detection, forecasting, document AI, vision counting, RAG, custom assistants.
    • Judge flagging tools on both precision (false alarms) and recall (missed issues).
    • Ask vendors for evidence on your own data, explainability, audit trails and data handling.
    • The skills that grow in value are supervising, verifying and interpreting AI.
