Audit essentials for a seven-warehouse network

AI is only as useful as the questions you ask it. This aisle sharpens the audit knowledge behind those questions: where data goes wrong, which measures matter, and which red flags to hunt.

Follow the flow: where errors creep in

Every number you audit was created at a step in this chain. When a figure looks wrong, ask which step produced it.

  1. 01RequisitionSomeone asks to buyUnapproved or split requests
  2. 02Purchase orderApproved order to supplierWrong price, PO after invoice
  3. 03Goods receiptGRN posted at the dockLate posting, wrong qty or UOM
  4. 04Putaway & stockStored in a locationMis-slotting, transfers in transit
  5. 05InvoiceSupplier bills youDuplicates, price or qty mismatch
  6. 06PaymentMoney goes outPaying without a match

Running alongside this flow are cycle counts and stock adjustments, inter-warehouse transfers, and returns. With seven warehouses, transfers and timing differences are a major source of apparent discrepancies that aren't real losses.

The three-way match

The core control in procurement: before paying, check that three documents agree.

Document 1

Purchase order

What we agreed to buy, at what price

Document 2

Goods receipt (GRN)

What we actually received

Document 3

Supplier invoice

What we're being asked to pay

If quantity and price agree within tolerance, the invoice is paid. If not, it becomes an exception for someone to resolve. Many AP systems now use AI to read invoices and match them automatically, routing only exceptions to people. Your audit job is to check that the matching is working and that exceptions are resolved properly, not waved through.

Measures that matter

These are the numbers you should be able to calculate and explain for each warehouse.

MeasureFormulaWhat it tells you
Inventory record accuracy (IRA)Lines within tolerance ÷ lines countedHow far you can trust the system's stock figures
Value-weighted varianceΣ |variance qty × unit cost| ÷ total value countedWhether errors are on cheap or expensive items
Net vs gross varianceNet = Σ variance; Gross = Σ |variance|A small net figure can hide large offsetting errors
Shrink rateValue lost (theft, damage, error) ÷ beginning inventory valueReal loss over a period
Cycle count completionCounts done ÷ counts scheduledWhether the counting programme is actually happening
Adjustment rateAdjustment lines ÷ total lines (or by user, by SKU)Frequent manual corrections are a risk signal
Match exception rateInvoices failing 3-way match ÷ invoices processedHealth of the purchase-to-pay process
PO complianceSpend with a valid PO ÷ total spendHow much buying bypasses the process
Benchmarks

Many customers in distribution, retail, CPG and healthcare expect inventory accuracy of 99% or higher, and disciplined cycle-counting programmes commonly reach above 98–99%. There is no single official standard, so set targets by warehouse and item class, then track each warehouse against its own trend.

Why stock records and counts disagree

Before calling something a loss, rule out the common, innocent causes. These are the usual suspects in multi-site networks:

CauseWhat it looks like in data
Timing gapsCount taken before a receipt or pick was posted. Variance reverses the next day.
System sync delaysWMS and ERP disagree for the same SKU and date.
Unit-of-measure mismatchVariance is an exact multiple (×6, ×12, ×24): cases counted as eaches or vice versa.
Transfers in transitStock leaves WH02 but hasn't been received at WH05. One shows a loss, the other a gain.
Inconsistent receivingOver- or under-receipts at one dock, often the same supplier or shift.
Mis-slottingShortage in one location, matching surplus in a nearby one for the same SKU.
Returns processingReturned items not put back, or put back without a system transaction.
Genuine lossDamage, theft or unrecorded scrap. Persistent, one-directional, often high-value items.

Count smarter with ABC classification

Not all items deserve equal attention. ABC classification ranks SKUs by annual value (cost × usage). Typically a small share of SKUs (the A items, often around 20%) make up most of the value (often around 80%). A common pattern is to count A items most often (for example monthly), B items less often, and C items least. Some teams also rank by velocity or risk, not just value. Module 8 shows how AI helps build a risk-based count list.

The red flag checklist

These patterns are widely cited by audit and fraud specialists. A red flag is a reason to look closer, not proof of wrongdoing. Most turn out to be process errors.

Invoices & payments

  • Same supplier, amount and date on two invoices
  • Near-duplicates: invoice number with a suffix ("4471-A"), or digits transposed
  • Invoices without a PO
  • Invoice quantity higher than goods received
  • Round-number invoices from service suppliers
  • PO created after the invoice date

Purchasing

  • Split POs: several orders just under an approval limit, same supplier, close dates
  • Spend with non-approved suppliers ("maverick spend")
  • Prices above contract or above the last PO
  • Repeated awards to one supplier without justification
  • Frequent change orders on the same supplier

Vendor master

  • Duplicate suppliers (same name, address, tax ID or bank account)
  • Missing tax ID or contact details
  • Supplier address or phone matching an employee's
  • PO box-only addresses
  • Recently created supplier with large, fast payments

Stock & adjustments

  • Repeated adjustments on the same SKU or by the same user
  • Adjustments just before or after a count
  • Adjustments outside working hours
  • Negative stock balances
  • Write-offs concentrated on high-value, portable items

Key takeaways

  • Trace every odd number back to the step that created it: PO, GRN, putaway, invoice, payment, count.
  • Track IRA, value-weighted and gross variance per warehouse, against each warehouse's own trend.
  • Rule out timing, UOM, transfers and mis-slotting before calling anything a loss.
  • Red flags are leads, not conclusions.

Check your understanding

WH03 shows a shortage of exactly 24 units on a SKU sold in cases of 12. What is the most likely first explanation?

Variances that are exact multiples of a pack size strongly suggest a UOM problem. Check before assuming loss.

Net variance for a warehouse is close to zero. Does that mean its records are accurate?

Offsetting errors hide in net figures. Gross variance and line-level accuracy show the real picture.

Four POs to one supplier on the same day, each between $4,900 and $4,990, with a $5,000 approval limit. What is this?

Clustering just under a limit is a classic split-PO pattern. Investigate, but it may have an innocent explanation.
