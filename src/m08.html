Audit playbooks: step-by-step AI routines

This is the heart of the course. Six routines that cover the core of a multi-warehouse audit, each with the data you need, the steps, copy-ready prompts and how to verify. Then you get to practise on a sample dataset.

Time 45 minLevel Intermediate → AdvancedHands-on Audit lab + quiz

By the end of this aisle you will be able to

  • Run a monthly variance review across all seven warehouses with AI support
  • Hunt for duplicate invoices, split POs and vendor master problems
  • Build a risk-based cycle count list and classify free-text adjustment reasons

Each playbook follows the same shape so you can learn the pattern: prepare the data → let AI find candidates → verify each one → record the finding. AI does the searching and sorting; you do the confirming.

P1 · Monthly multi-warehouse variance review

GoalFind where stock records are least reliable and why
WhenMonthly, after cycle counts close
DataCycle count results for all 7 warehouses (combined via Power Query)
OutputOne-page summary + exception list per warehouse
  1. Refresh and tie out. Refresh your Power Query table. Check row counts per warehouse against what each site says it counted.
  2. Calculate the measures. Var_Qty, Var_Pct, Var_Value, Out_Of_Tolerance per line (formulas from Module 7). PivotTable: IRA, net and gross variance value by warehouse.
  3. Ask AI to find patterns. Upload the anonymised result, or use Copilot in the workbook, with the prompt below.
  4. Verify the top patterns. For each pattern AI suggests, filter the rows yourself. Does it hold? Is there an innocent cause (timing, UOM, transfer)?
  5. Compare to last month. Which warehouses improved or worsened? Trend matters more than one month.
  6. Draft the summary with AI (Module 9), then edit.
You are an inventory audit analyst. Attached are this month's cycle count results for 7 warehouses (data dictionary below). Tolerance is ±2% per line.
Using code:
1. Confirm row count per warehouse and total gross variance value.
2. For each warehouse: IRA %, net variance value, gross variance value.
3. Find patterns in out-of-tolerance lines: by SKU, ABC class, UOM, count date, counter, location zone.
4. Flag variances that are exact multiples of pack sizes (possible UOM errors) and SKUs with opposite variances in two warehouses on similar dates (possible transfers in transit).
5. List the 15 highest-value exceptions.
Present results as tables. For each pattern, show the supporting row count. Don't draw conclusions about causes the data can't support.

Verify

Tie AI's totals to your PivotTable. Open 5 of the top exceptions in the WMS to confirm system quantities at the count date.

P2 · Duplicate and near-duplicate invoice hunt

GoalCatch invoices paid, or about to be paid, twice
WhenMonthly, or before a payment run
DataInvoice register: supplier ID, invoice no., date, amount, PO no., warehouse
OutputList of suspected duplicates with evidence

Exact duplicates (same supplier and invoice number) are usually blocked by the system. The ones that slip through are near-duplicates: an invoice number with a suffix or extra character, digits swapped, the same amount on a slightly different date, or the same invoice entered under two supplier records.

  1. Exact pass in Excel. COUNTIFS on Supplier + Amount + Date. Anything over 1 is a candidate (see the Module 7 formula lab).
  2. Normalise invoice numbers. Ask AI for a formula that strips spaces, dashes, leading zeros and letter suffixes, so "INV-04471-A" and "4471" compare as equal.
  3. Fuzzy pass with AI. Upload the anonymised register with the prompt below.
  4. Review each candidate against the actual invoice documents. Credit notes and genuine repeat orders explain many matches.
  5. Record and escalate confirmed duplicates to AP for recovery or blocking.
Using code, analyse this invoice register for possible duplicate invoices. Flag pairs where ANY of these is true:
a) Same supplier and same amount within 30 days
b) Invoice numbers identical after removing spaces, dashes, leading zeros and trailing letters
c) Invoice numbers that differ by one character or have two adjacent digits swapped, same supplier
d) Same amount and invoice number but different supplier IDs (possible duplicate vendor)
Exclude negative amounts (credit notes). For each flagged pair, show both rows side by side and which rule triggered. Sort by amount, highest first. Tell me how many rows you analysed.
Verify

Expect false positives: monthly fixed fees and standing orders produce identical amounts legitimately. Check each against the PO and GRN.

P3 · Split purchase order and threshold test

GoalFind orders split to stay under approval limits
WhenQuarterly
DataPO register: PO no., date, supplier, requester, warehouse, value. Your approval limits
OutputClusters of suspicious POs with requester and approver
  1. List your approval thresholds (for example $5,000 / $20,000 / $50,000).
  2. Band the POs. Flag POs between 90% and 100% of each threshold.
  3. Cluster. Same supplier + same requester (or warehouse) within 7 days, whose combined value exceeds the threshold.
  4. Optional: first-digit test. See the note below.
  5. Review each cluster: was there a business reason for separate orders?
Our PO approval limits are [$5,000, $20,000, $50,000]. Using code on the attached PO register:
1. Count POs falling within 90–100% of each limit, by warehouse and requester. Compare to POs within 80–90% (a much higher count just under a limit is suspicious).
2. Find clusters: same supplier AND same requester, POs within 7 days of each other, each below a limit but together above it.
3. For each cluster, list the POs, dates, values, combined total and the limit avoided.
4. Rank requesters by number of clusters.
Present as tables. These are leads for review, not conclusions.
About Benford's law

In many natural financial datasets, leading digits follow a known pattern (1 appears about 30% of the time, 9 under 5%). Auditors test purchase data against it. In one published case, BDO's first-two-digit test found one person processing an unusual number of POs starting 15–19, just under their $20,000 authority limit. Caveats from ISACA: use it on large datasets (sets of 500 or fewer are generally unsuitable), it doesn't fit data with built-in minimums or maximums, and a mismatch is a reason to look, not proof. Ask AI: Run a first-two-digit Benford test on PO values and chart actual vs expected.

P4 · Vendor master health check

GoalA clean, trustworthy supplier list
WhenTwice a year, and after any system migration
DataVendor master: ID, name, address, tax ID, bank (masked), created date, status, last transaction
OutputDuplicates, incomplete records, dormant vendors, risk flags
Data sensitivity

Vendor master data is often red-light data (bank details, tax IDs). Mask bank accounts to the last 4 digits and use only your company-approved tool, or ask AI for the formulas and run them yourself.

  1. Standardise names. Remove "Ltd", "Inc", "Pvt", punctuation and extra spaces, convert to upper case. AI can write this formula.
  2. Find duplicates on standardised name, tax ID, address, phone and masked bank account.
  3. Check completeness: missing tax ID, address or contact.
  4. Find dormant vendors with no transactions in 12–18 months. Candidates for blocking.
  5. Cross-check vendor addresses and bank details against the employee list (with HR or Internal Audit, given sensitivity).
Using code, check this anonymised vendor master for data quality:
1. Create a standardised name (upper case, remove punctuation and words like LTD, LIMITED, INC, PVT, CO, CORP).
2. Flag likely duplicates: same standardised name, OR same tax ID, OR same masked bank account, OR very similar names (e.g. "NORTHLINE PALLETS" vs "NORTH LINE PALLET").
3. Flag records missing tax ID, address or phone.
4. Flag vendors with no transaction in 18 months, and vendors created in the last 90 days with total payments above [$X].
Return one table per check with vendor IDs, and a summary count for each.

P5 · Risk-based cycle count plan

GoalCount where errors are most likely and most costly
WhenMonthly planning
DataSKU list with value, movement, past variance history, adjustment count, location
OutputPrioritised count list per warehouse

Instead of counting on a fixed rotation, give each SKU-location a risk score. AI-powered inventory systems do this automatically, using variance history, transaction volume and item value. You can build a simple version yourself.

  1. Pick 4–5 risk factors: ABC value class, number of transactions last month, past variance count, adjustments, days since last count.
  2. Ask AI to design a scoring formula (prompt below). Keep it simple enough to explain to warehouse managers.
  3. Sort and select the top N per warehouse based on counting capacity.
  4. Review after 2–3 months. Are risk-based counts finding more variances than random ones? Adjust weights.
Help me design a simple risk score (0–100) to prioritise cycle counts across 7 warehouses. I have these columns per SKU-location: ABC_Class, Unit_Cost, Txn_Count_30d, Variance_Count_6m, Adj_Count_6m, Days_Since_Last_Count.
1. Suggest weights for each factor with a one-line reason.
2. Give me the Excel formula for the score (Microsoft 365, Excel Table named Items).
3. Give me a formula to list the top 40 locations per warehouse.
Keep it explainable to a warehouse manager in two sentences.

P6 · Classify free-text adjustment reasons

GoalTurn messy comments into categories you can count
WhenMonthly, alongside P1
DataStock adjustment log with a free-text reason column
OutputAdjustments by root-cause category, by warehouse

Adjustment comments like "dmgd in rack", "recount - found in A-12", "case/each issue" are hard to analyse. Language is exactly what AI is good at.

  1. Agree categories such as Damage, Count error, Mis-slot found, UOM, Transfer timing, Receiving error, Unknown.
  2. Classify. Use =COPILOT() in a new column, or upload the log to an assistant with the prompt below.
  3. Spot-check 30 rows. Adjust categories or instructions if the AI is confused.
  4. Pivot category by warehouse. "Unknown" and "Count error" concentration point to where the process needs attention.
Classify each stock adjustment reason below into exactly one category:
Damage | Count error | Mis-slot found | UOM error | Transfer timing | Receiving error | Unknown
Rules: if the reason is vague (e.g. "adj", "per mgr"), use Unknown. Don't guess.
Examples:
"dmgd forklift" → Damage
"found in B-04 not A-04" → Mis-slot found
"cs vs ea" → UOM error
Return a table: Row_ID, Reason, Category, Confidence (High/Low).

Key takeaways

  • Every playbook follows the same loop: prepare → AI finds candidates → you verify → record.
  • Near-duplicates and threshold clustering are where AI adds most beyond basic system checks.
  • Language tasks like classifying adjustment reasons are a perfect fit for AI.
  • Treat every AI flag as a lead. Confirm against source documents before calling it a finding.

Check your understanding

AI flags two invoices from the same supplier for $2,400.00 in consecutive months. What next?

Recurring charges produce identical amounts legitimately. Verify against source documents before calling it a finding.

Why is the "normalise invoice numbers" step important?

Systems usually block exact duplicates. Near-duplicates with formatting differences are the ones that get paid twice.
