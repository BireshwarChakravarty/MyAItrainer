Reporting findings

This module covers writing up what you find: structuring a finding, adapting it for different readers, and picking the right chart.

About 30 minutes. Level: Intermediate.

After this module you should be able to:

  • Write an audit finding using the five-part structure auditors use
  • Adapt the same finding for a warehouse manager, your manager and leadership
  • Choose the right chart for the point you're making

The five parts of a finding

Internal auditors structure findings around five elements. Using them makes your findings complete, fair and hard to argue with.

ElementQuestionExample
ConditionWhat did you find?38 of 418 count lines at WH05 (9.1%) were outside ±2% tolerance in October.
CriteriaWhat should it be?Network target is 98% accuracy; company SOP requires UOM confirmation at receiving.
CauseWhy did it happen?22 of the 38 lines were case-pack SKUs received as eaches; WH05's receiving screen defaults to EA.
EffectWhy does it matter?Gross variance of $14,300; risk of stock-outs and over-ordering on affected SKUs.
Corrective actionWhat should happen, by whom, by when?WMS team to change default UOM by 30 Nov; WH05 supervisor to recount the 22 SKUs this week.

Which SKUs, which locations, what to do this week. Collaborative tone. Never names individuals as at fault.

Your manager

Leadership

Three lines

On tone

Findings describe processes, not people. "Receiving screen defaults to EA" invites a fix. "WH05 staff keep making mistakes" invites defensiveness. Add to any prompt: Use neutral, factual language. Focus on process causes, not individuals.

You are helping me write the monthly inventory audit summary for my manager. Here are my notes and results for 7 warehouses:
[paste IRA by warehouse, this month and last month]
[paste top 3–5 findings in note form]
Write:
1. A 3-sentence headline summary (network accuracy, trend, biggest issue).
2. A short table: warehouse, IRA this month, change vs last month, status (On target / Watch / Action).
3. The top findings in Condition / Cause / Effect / Action format, max 4 lines each.
4. What I need from my manager (decisions or support).
Plain business English, no jargon, no exaggeration. Use only the numbers I've given you.
Draft a short, friendly email to the WH[0X] warehouse manager about this month's count results.
Facts: [paste].
I want them to: [recount these SKUs / check receiving UOM settings / reply with explanations by date].
Tone: collaborative, specific, no blame. Under 150 words. End with a clear ask and deadline.

Pick the right chart

Your pointUseAvoid
Comparing 7 warehousesHorizontal bar chart, sorted, with a target linePie charts (hard to compare 7 slices)
Trend over monthsLine chart, one line per warehouse or just the worst threeSeven-colour spaghetti charts
Where the value isSorted bar or Pareto chart of variance value by SKU3D effects of any kind
A list of exceptionsA table with conditional formattingA chart when people need the detail

Ask AI: I want to show [point] to [audience]. My data has [columns]. What chart should I use and how should I set it up in Excel?

Summary

  • Structure every finding: Condition, Criteria, Cause, Effect, Corrective action.
  • Let AI adapt one finding for each audience. You keep the facts fixed.
  • Describe processes, not people.
  • Bar charts for comparing warehouses, lines for trends, tables for exceptions.

Take one real issue from your recent audits. Run it through the finding builder, polish it with AI, and compare it to how you would normally have written it. Use the improved version in your next report.

Quick quiz

Which element answers "why did this happen?"

Cause is the root reason. Effect is the consequence, and Condition is what you observed.

Which phrasing is better in a finding?

Process-focused language is factual and invites a fix rather than defensiveness.

Best chart to compare accuracy across seven warehouses?

Sorted bars make ranking obvious, and the target line shows who is on or off track instantly.
