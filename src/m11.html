Six technologies worth understanding

Predictive

Anomaly detection

The system learns what "normal" looks like (typical receipts per day, typical adjustments per shift, a supplier's usual invoice pattern) and flags what deviates. No one writes a rule for each case. Good tools present flags as leads for review, not accusations.

Predictive

Demand forecasting

Learns patterns such as seasonality, trends and promotions from sales history to predict future demand per SKU and warehouse. Directly affects purchasing quantities and safety stock.

Vision & documents

Document AI (OCR+)

Reads scanned invoices, delivery notes and GRNs, and extracts fields like invoice number, date, lines and totals. This powers automatic three-way matching.

Vision

Computer vision counting

Cameras, drones or robots scan racks and count stock continuously rather than in periodic cycle counts. Gives a near-live view of inventory, mostly in large or high-value sites.

Generative

RAG: AI that reads your documents

"Retrieval-augmented generation": before answering, the AI searches your company documents (SOPs, contracts, policies) and answers from them, citing sources. This is how "ask our procurement policy" assistants work, and it greatly reduces made-up answers.

Generative

Custom assistants and agents

An assistant preconfigured with your instructions and files (a Custom GPT, Claude Project, Copilot agent or Gemini Gem). For example, an "Audit helper" loaded with your SOPs, data dictionary and report template.

How to judge whether an AI tool is accurate

Vendors often quote one accuracy figure. For audit tools that flag problems, you need two:

Precision

Of everything it flagged, how much was really a problem? Low precision means your team wastes time on false alarms and starts ignoring the tool.

Recall

Of all the real problems, how many did it catch? Low recall means real issues slip through while everyone assumes they're covered.

Questions to ask any AI vendor

Whether it's a new procurement platform, a WMS add-on or an AP automation tool, these questions separate real capability from marketing:

Accuracy

  • What are the precision and recall on data like ours? How was it measured?
  • How much historical data does it need? (Some invoice-matching tools cite 6–12 months.)
  • Can we run a pilot on our own data before committing?

Explainability & control

  • When it flags something, does it show why?
  • Can we set thresholds and approval rules?
  • Is every automated action logged in an audit trail?

Data & security

  • Where is our data stored? Is it used to train models for other customers?
  • What security certifications do you hold?
  • Who can access our data on your side?

Fit

  • Does it integrate with our ERP and WMS? How?
  • What does our team have to do differently day to day?
  • What does it cost in year two and three, not just year one?
Be careful with vendor figures

Much of what's written about AI in procurement comes from vendors. Figures like "99% detection" or "60% less counting labour" are often unverified or measured in ideal conditions. Ask for the source, and test on your own data.

Build your own audit helper

A practical advanced step: create a custom assistant (if your tool supports Projects, GPTs, Gems or Copilot agents) that already knows your audit work.

  1. Gather files (anonymised where needed): your data dictionary, audit SOPs, tolerance rules, finding template, a good past report.
  2. Write instructions combining your Module 4 custom instructions with: "Answer from the attached documents where possible and say which document. If the documents don't cover it, say so."
  3. Test it with 5 questions you already know the answers to. Fix the instructions where it goes wrong.
  4. Share it with colleagues if your tool allows. Consistent methods across the team are valuable.

Where things are heading

  • Adoption is broad but shallow. EFESO's 2026 research found only about 5% of procurement organisations have truly scaled generative AI. People who can turn experiments into reliable routines are in demand.
  • Agents are arriving in procurement. Gartner forecasts agentic AI spending in supply chain software rising from under $2 billion in 2025 to about $53 billion by 2030 (as reported in trade press).
  • Roles are shifting. In a Gartner survey of supply chain leaders, 55% expect agentic AI to reduce hiring for entry-level positions. Routine checking will be automated; supervising, verifying and interpreting will be valued more.

Summary

  • Know the main technologies: anomaly detection, forecasting, document AI, vision counting, RAG, custom assistants.
  • Judge flagging tools on both precision (false alarms) and recall (missed issues).
  • Ask vendors for evidence on your own data, explainability, audit trails and data handling.
  • The skills that grow in value are supervising, verifying and interpreting AI.

Quick quiz

A tool flags 200 invoices; 10 are real duplicates. What is the problem?

Precision = 10 ÷ 200 = 5%. Your team would waste time and soon stop trusting the tool.

What does RAG let an AI assistant do?

Retrieval-augmented generation searches your documents first and grounds the answer in them, reducing made-up answers.

A vendor claims "99% accuracy". What's the best response?

A single accuracy figure can hide a lot. Evidence on your own data is what counts.
